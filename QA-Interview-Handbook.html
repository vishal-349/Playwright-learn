QA Interview Handbook

Manual + Automation QA Engineer · 3–4 Years Experience

Prepared for hiring panels conducting technical rounds for mid-level QA positions.
49 scenario-based questions across 8 competency areas, with expected answers, follow-ups, red flags, and differentiators.

How to Use This Handbook

This is not a question bank to read aloud end-to-end. A single round should cover 8–12 questions drawn from the sections most relevant to the role. The value of these questions lies in the follow-ups — a memorised answer collapses on the second probe, which is exactly what the follow-ups are designed to expose.

Interviewing technique for this level:

  • Always ask for a specific instance. If a candidate answers in general terms ("we usually check the logs"), respond with "Tell me about the last time you actually did that." Real experience produces details: a ticket number, a service name, a specific wrong value.
  • Let silence work. Strong candidates think before answering. Rehearsed candidates start talking immediately and fluently.
  • Do not correct them mid-answer. A wrong turn that the candidate catches and recovers from is a stronger signal than a right answer delivered without reasoning.
  • Score the process, not the vocabulary. A candidate who says "I would compare the network tab response against what the component expects" is stronger than one who recites "root cause analysis using the five whys methodology."

The expected answers below are written at the level of a genuinely strong 3–4 year engineer. Treat them as the upper end of the band, not a pass mark.

1. Project Deep Dive

This section is the single most effective filter in the entire interview. A candidate who has genuinely worked on an enterprise application can describe it as a system — users, data, integrations, failure modes. A candidate who has not will describe it as a list of features. Spend 15–20 minutes here before moving on; the answers will also tell you which later sections are worth pursuing.

Q1. Walk me through the application you tested most recently. Who are its users, what business problem does it solve, and which part of it was the most difficult to test?

Expected Answer

A strong candidate answers as a domain participant, not a feature reader. They set the context first — for example, "It is a lending platform used by three groups: field sales agents who submit loan applications, credit officers who approve them, and an operations team that handles disbursement. About 400 internal users, roughly 2,000 applications a day." Then they explain the money-critical or compliance-critical path and why it was hard: multi-stage approval workflows where the same record moves through several states, each with different field-level permissions, so the test matrix is role × state rather than a flat set of screens.

They should name the concrete difficulty rather than saying "it was complex." Good examples: date-sensitive interest calculation that could only be validated by recomputing expected values in SQL; a third-party credit bureau integration that was rate-limited in QA, so they had to work with stubbed responses and periodically validate against the real sandbox; or asynchronous document processing where the UI showed "processing" and the actual result arrived by webhook minutes later, which broke naive test flows.

They should also mention what they owned specifically versus what the team owned — a real engineer knows the boundaries of their own work.

Follow-up 1: Which module of that application broke most often, and why do you think it kept breaking?

Expected: They should name a specific module and give a structural reason, not blame. Typical strong answers: "The pricing module, because three teams wrote to the same configuration table and there was no single owner"; or "The reports module, because it read from a replica that lagged behind the primary, so any test that wrote data and immediately verified a report was unreliable." The reasoning matters more than the module. Weak candidates say "the login module" or "whatever the developers changed last."

Follow-up 2: If you had to hand this application to a new QA engineer tomorrow, what would you tell them that is not written in any document?

Expected: This is the hardest question to fake. Real answers are oddly specific and slightly unglamorous: "Never test refunds with the shared QA account, three people use it and the balance changes under you"; "The staging cron runs at 2 AM, so any date-based test done in the evening will look wrong until the next morning"; "Two customers have custom configuration flags, so a bug that reproduces for them may not reproduce on a default tenant." Tribal knowledge of this kind only comes from time spent inside a system.

Red Flags

  • Describes the application only as a feature list ("it had login, dashboard, reports, admin panel") with no users, volumes, or business purpose.
  • Cannot name a single integration, downstream system, or data source.
  • Uses vague scale words — "it was a very big application," "we had a huge database" — with no numbers when asked.
  • Every difficulty is attributed to other people: unclear requirements, bad developers, no time.
  • Answers the "not written anywhere" follow-up with a process statement ("I would tell them to read the test cases carefully").

Strong Candidate Indicators

  • Volunteers the business risk of the module they tested — what happens to the company if it fails, not just what happens on screen.
  • Knows where the data lives: which tables, which service owns them, which reports read from them.
  • Distinguishes between "hard to test because the domain is complicated" and "hard to test because the environment was unreliable," and can speak to both.
  • Mentions something they changed about how the team tested it, not just what they tested.

Q2. Tell me about the most serious bug that reached production on your watch. What was it, how was it found, and what changed afterwards?

Expected Answer

The strongest version of this answer is honest and structural. The candidate describes a real escape with real consequences — duplicate charges to customers, a report showing wrong totals to a regulator, an email campaign sent to the wrong segment — and does not minimise it.

They should cover four things. What the bug was technically: for example, an idempotency gap where a retried payment request created a second order because the deduplication key included a timestamp. How it was found: usually by a customer, support ticket, or monitoring alert — and a mature candidate admits this rather than claiming QA caught everything. Why testing missed it: the honest reasons are recognisable — it only occurred under slow network conditions, or with a data shape that did not exist in QA, or in a flow that crossed two teams' boundaries and neither team's test suite covered the seam. What changed: a regression test added at the right layer, a monitoring alert, a change to the release checklist, or a data-setup improvement in QA.

Crucially, a strong candidate frames the fix at the process level, not as "I will be more careful next time."

Follow-up 1: Was the gap in your test coverage, your test data, or your environment? Be specific.

Expected: They should choose and defend one. "Coverage" means the scenario was never conceived — a design gap. "Data" means the scenario was conceived but no realistic data existed to exercise it — extremely common in enterprise work, e.g. QA had no account with a negative balance. "Environment" means the QA setup differed materially from production — single instance versus load-balanced, caching disabled, third party stubbed. A candidate who can classify their own miss is thinking like an engineer.

Follow-up 2: Did the regression test you added actually catch anything since? How would you know if it was useful?

Expected: An excellent candidate is comfortable saying "honestly, no — it has never failed since, and I have wondered whether it is earning its runtime." That is real engineering judgement. They may add that they check whether a test has ever failed for a genuine reason before defending its place in a slow suite, and that some post-incident tests are better replaced by a production alert because the condition cannot be reliably created in QA at all.

Red Flags

  • "No major bug ever escaped from my testing." This is not possible in three years of enterprise work and signals either inexperience or dishonesty.
  • The example is trivial — a spelling mistake, a misaligned button — presented as serious.
  • Blames the developer or the deadline with no reflection on the testing gap.
  • Describes the fix purely as "we did more regression testing after that."
  • Cannot explain the technical mechanism of the bug, only its symptom.

Strong Candidate Indicators

  • Owns the miss without excessive self-blame, and separates the human factor from the system factor.
  • Recognises that some classes of bug are better caught by monitoring than by tests, and can say which.
  • Mentions the detection-time problem, not just the prevention problem — how long the bug ran before anyone noticed.
  • Has an opinion on whether the post-incident process change actually stuck or quietly died.

Q3. Describe your test data setup on that project. How did you get an account or record into the exact state you needed to test?

Expected Answer

Test data is where genuine experience is impossible to fake, because every real project has an ugly story here. A strong candidate describes a layered approach and is honest about the mess.

They typically cover: seeded baseline data restored or refreshed on a schedule, and the pain that comes when it drifts; API-based setup, where they create the precondition through service calls rather than clicking through the UI, because a five-step UI flow to reach the state under test is slow and itself a source of failure; direct database seeding for states that no API exposes, with the caveat that this can create records the application itself would never produce and therefore mask or invent bugs; and dedicated data per test or per run with unique identifiers so parallel execution does not collide.

They should name the specific hard cases: data that cannot easily be reset, such as a KYC-verified customer or a closed financial period; data with time dependencies, such as an account 90 days overdue; and data owned by a third party, such as a payment gateway's record of a settled transaction. Good candidates mention masked or synthetic production-like data and why raw production copies are a compliance problem.

Follow-up 1: What happens to your test data when two engineers or two parallel test runs need the same record?

Expected: They should immediately recognise this as a collision problem, and describe generating unique data per run (timestamped or UUID-suffixed emails, order references, tenant identifiers) rather than sharing fixtures. For genuinely scarce resources — a single sandbox merchant account, a limited pool of test cards — they describe serialising those specific tests, using a locking or checkout mechanism, or dedicating a worker. A candidate who has never hit this problem has never run tests in parallel or in a team.

Follow-up 2: You need to test what happens to an invoice 90 days after it was raised. How do you do that without waiting 90 days?

Expected: Multiple valid approaches, and a strong candidate weighs them. Backdate the record so the created date is 90 days in the past — simple, but only works if all downstream logic reads that field and no audit trail contradicts it. Manipulate the environment clock — effective but disruptive to everyone sharing the environment, and it breaks certificates and third-party calls. Trigger the scheduled job manually against seeded data — usually the cleanest. Or push the date logic down to a unit-level test and validate only the trigger integration end-to-end. The best answer names the trade-off, not just the trick.

Red Flags

  • "We used the same test user for everything" with no awareness of the problems that causes.
  • Has never used an API or database to set up state; every precondition was created by clicking through the UI.
  • No awareness that copying production data raises privacy or compliance concerns.
  • Cannot describe a single case where the required data state was genuinely hard to produce.
  • Believes writing directly to the database is always safe and equivalent to real application behaviour.

Strong Candidate Indicators

  • Treats test data as a design problem with a cost, not an incidental chore.
  • Knows the risk of database seeding: it can create states the application cannot produce, generating false bugs and hiding real ones.
  • Distinguishes data that must be fresh per test from data that can be shared read-only.
  • Has automated some part of data setup and can explain what it saved.

Q4. Take one feature you tested and walk me through how you decided what to test — from reading the requirement to signing off.

Expected Answer

Look for a reasoning process, not a recital of test design techniques. A strong candidate starts before the test cases: they read the requirement and identify what is missing from it. "The story said a user can apply a discount code. It did not say what happens if the code expires between adding it to the cart and completing payment, whether it stacks with an existing promotion, or whether it applies before or after tax. I raised those three questions in refinement, and two of them turned out to be genuine product gaps."

Then they describe deriving coverage from risk, not from symmetry: which paths involve money, data loss, permissions, or external systems get depth; cosmetic paths get a pass. They should mention identifying the boundaries and equivalence classes concretely — not naming the technique, but using it: minimum and maximum order values, exactly-at-expiry, one currency versus another. They should cover negative and permission cases, integration points, and the states the feature can be entered from.

For sign-off, a mature answer includes what they deliberately did not test and communicated as residual risk, plus the checks they ran after deployment. Sign-off as an informed risk statement rather than a clean-sheet declaration is the mark of experience.

Follow-up 1: The requirement was ambiguous. What exactly did you do, and how long did you wait for an answer?

Expected: Real engineers do not block indefinitely. Strong answers: they wrote down their assumption explicitly in the ticket, tested against it, flagged it in the daily standup, and made the decision visible so that if the assumption was wrong the cost was known. They may describe checking how the existing system already behaves, since consistency with current behaviour is often the intended answer. They should also name who they went to — product owner, business analyst, the developer, or a support person who knows what customers actually do.

Follow-up 2: You had two days for something that needed five. What did you cut, and how did you communicate it?

Expected: They should cut by risk, and be able to articulate the ranking. Keep: the primary revenue or data-integrity path, permissions, and anything touching an integration. Defer: exhaustive cross-browser checks, edge combinations in low-traffic paths, cosmetic verification, and rarely used administrative screens. The communication half matters as much: a written statement of what was and was not covered, sent to the people who own the release decision, so that shipping becomes their informed choice. A candidate who says "I worked late and tested everything" has missed the point of the question.

Red Flags

  • Jumps straight to "I write positive and negative test cases" without engaging with the specific feature.
  • Recites test design technique names (boundary value analysis, decision table) without applying them to a real example.
  • Never questioned a requirement; treats the specification as complete and authoritative.
  • Sign-off means "all test cases passed," with no notion of residual risk.
  • Cannot prioritise under time pressure other than by working longer hours.

Strong Candidate Indicators

  • Found requirement gaps before development started and can cite the specific questions they asked.
  • Prioritises by business and data risk with reasoning, and can defend what they chose to leave untested.
  • Thinks about the states a feature can be entered from, not just the happy path through it.
  • Treats sign-off as communicating risk to a decision-maker rather than as personal certification.

Q5. What did the release process look like on your project, and what was your role on release day?

Expected Answer

A candidate who has actually shipped enterprise software knows this in operational detail. They describe the cadence honestly — fortnightly releases with a code freeze, or continuous deployment with feature flags — and their specific responsibilities within it.

Expect concrete mechanics: a regression cycle before freeze and what it consisted of; how the build moved through environments (development, QA, staging or UAT, production) and what specifically differed between staging and production, because a real engineer knows staging is never identical; who signed off and on what evidence; the deployment window and why it was chosen; and the post-deployment verification they personally ran — a short smoke set against production covering login, one read path, one write path, one payment or integration path.

Strong candidates also describe the failure branch: what triggered a rollback decision, who could call it, and whether rollback was genuinely possible. Many enterprise systems cannot roll back cleanly once a database migration has run, and a candidate who knows this has lived through it. They may mention feature flags as the mitigation, and the coordination overhead of releasing alongside mobile clients or partner integrations that cannot be updated simultaneously.

Follow-up 1: A release went out and something looked wrong. Walk me through the next thirty minutes.

Expected: Sequence and judgement. Establish blast radius first — how many users, which flows, is money or data being corrupted. Confirm it is real and caused by this release, not a coincidental third-party outage: check error rates and logs against the deployment timestamp. Communicate early to the release channel rather than investigating silently. Then the decision: roll back, disable via feature flag, or fix forward. A strong candidate says explicitly that data corruption changes the calculus — you stop the write path immediately, because every additional minute increases the cleanup cost.

Follow-up 2: How did you decide what went into the smoke set you ran after deployment?

Expected: Deployment smoke tests answer "is the system wired up correctly," not "is the logic correct." So: the application loads and serves authenticated requests, database connectivity works, each critical downstream integration responds, configuration and secrets loaded for the right environment, and one end-to-end write completes. Kept deliberately small — ten to fifteen minutes — because its purpose is a fast go/no-go signal. Strong candidates mention that most infrastructure and configuration failures show up here, which is precisely why the set is weighted towards connectivity rather than business rules.

Red Flags

  • Was never involved in a release; testing simply ended and "the team deployed it."
  • Believes staging and production are identical.
  • No concept of rollback, feature flags, or post-deployment verification.
  • Describes running the full regression suite against production after every release.
  • Cannot name who made the go/no-go decision or on what basis.

Strong Candidate Indicators

  • Knows the specific ways their staging environment differed from production and which bug classes that hid.
  • Understands that database migrations constrain rollback, and has seen this constrain a real decision.
  • Has a deliberate, small production smoke set with a stated purpose.
  • Treats release-day communication as part of the job, not an interruption to it.

Q6. Tell me about a time you disagreed with a developer about whether something was a bug. How did it end?

Expected Answer

This tests evidence-based reasoning and professional maturity together. A strong candidate describes a specific disagreement and, importantly, is willing to describe one where they turned out to be wrong.

The pattern to look for: they reduced the argument to evidence rather than opinion. "He said the API was returning the correct value and the problem was on my side. I sent him the exact request, the raw response, the record identifier, and the requirement line that specified the expected rounding. It turned out the API was correct and the UI was rounding a second time, so the ticket moved to the front-end team." Or, in the other direction: "I raised it as a bug, he showed me the acceptance criteria explicitly allowed that behaviour, and I withdrew it and instead raised it with the product owner as a usability concern — which was eventually accepted as a change request."

Expect them to distinguish three genuinely different situations: a defect against specification, correct behaviour that is nonetheless bad for the user, and a gap in the specification itself. Each has a different escalation path. Escalating to a lead or product owner should be framed as clarifying a decision, not as winning an argument.

Follow-up 1: What if it is genuinely not-reproducible for the developer but you have seen it three times?

Expected: They should treat non-reproducibility as information rather than a verdict. Systematically compare the environments: browser and version, user role and permissions, tenant or account configuration, data state of the specific record, environment, timing, cache state, and network conditions. Capture harder evidence — screen recording, HAR file, browser console, correlation identifier from the request so the developer can find the exact server-side log entry. A strong candidate says they have solved this by sitting with the developer and reproducing it on their own machine, and that the difference is almost always data or configuration rather than magic.

Follow-up 2: The developer marks it "won't fix" and the sprint ends. What do you do?

Expected: Mature answers accept that not every bug gets fixed, and focus on making the decision informed and traceable. Ensure the ticket records the impact, frequency, affected user group, and any workaround, so the decision is documented rather than lost. Route the decision to whoever owns priority — product owner or QA lead — rather than continuing to argue with the developer, who usually does not own that call. If it is a data-integrity or security issue, escalate with urgency and say so plainly. What you do not want to hear is either "I would keep reopening it" or "I would just let it go and move on."

Red Flags

  • Has never been wrong in such a disagreement.
  • Frames the story adversarially — developers as opponents, QA as the gatekeeper.
  • Escalates immediately without first gathering evidence.
  • Cannot distinguish "defect against specification" from "specification is wrong."
  • Reports bugs without request/response detail, identifiers, or environment information, then complains that developers close them.

Strong Candidate Indicators

  • Writes bug reports that make the developer's investigation faster: exact payload, record identifier, correlation identifier, environment, and expected-versus-actual with the source of "expected."
  • Comfortable being wrong publicly and correcting course without defensiveness.
  • Understands they influence priority rather than set it, and works accordingly.
  • Has converted a rejected bug into an accepted product improvement through the right channel.

2. Agile, Scrum and QA Process

Most candidates can recite the ceremonies. Very few can describe what actually happens when the process meets a real deadline. These questions target the friction points — incomplete stories, testing that does not fit inside the sprint, regression suites that outgrow the cycle, and estimates that were wrong.

Q7. Development finishes on the second-to-last day of the sprint. This happens every sprint. As the QA engineer, what do you actually do about it?

Expected Answer

A strong candidate treats this as a systemic problem and describes both the immediate coping strategy and the structural fix, while being realistic that they cannot mandate change alone.

Immediate tactics they should mention: getting involved much earlier — writing test conditions during refinement so no time is lost after handover; testing incrementally against partially complete work rather than waiting for a full feature (validate the API before the UI exists, test one role's flow while another is still in progress); pairing with the developer to run through the main path on their machine before the ticket is formally moved; and prioritising ruthlessly so the highest-risk paths get tested first rather than testing in ticket order and running out of time on the important one.

Structural points: raising the pattern in retrospective with data rather than as a complaint — "in the last four sprints, an average of 70 percent of story points moved to QA in the final two days, and we carried over three tickets each time." Proposing a smaller batch flow, a definition of done that includes testing, staggered handover so stories arrive throughout the sprint, or splitting stories so something testable lands on day three. A realistic candidate also acknowledges the honest outcome: sometimes the correct answer is that the sprint commitment was too large and carry-over is the symptom, not the disease.

Follow-up 1: The developer asks you to "just do a quick check" so the ticket can be closed today. What do you say?

Expected: Not a flat refusal, and not silent compliance. A strong answer: do a focused check on the highest-risk path, be explicit about what was and was not covered, and record that in the ticket. "I have verified the main flow for an admin user on Chrome. I have not checked the non-admin permissions or the failure path on the payment call." Then let the team decide whether that is enough to close. The principle is that the QA engineer's job is to make the coverage state visible, not to grant or withhold permission.

Follow-up 2: You raised it in retrospective and nothing changed. Now what?

Expected: Realism without cynicism. Options a real engineer uses: make the cost visible in a form leadership reacts to — escaped defects traced to rushed testing, carry-over trends, time spent on rework; find one narrow change the team will accept rather than a process overhaul, such as agreeing that one story must reach QA by day three; work with individual developers where the relationship is good and let the practice spread; automate the regression burden so the crunch is less damaging even if it persists. A candidate who has been in a team for two years knows that gradual, negotiated change works and that repeating the same retrospective complaint does not.

Red Flags

  • "I would tell them QA needs more time" with no alternative plan.
  • Believes they can block a release unilaterally, or that this is their role.
  • Never starts testing until a ticket is formally moved to a testing column.
  • Has no view on how to reduce risk when time is genuinely short.
  • Presents this as purely a developer discipline problem with no QA-side adaptation.

Strong Candidate Indicators

  • Shifts their own work earlier in the cycle instead of only asking others to change.
  • Tests below the UI when the UI is not ready yet.
  • Brings data to retrospectives rather than sentiment.
  • Understands the difference between a delivery-capacity problem and a testing-time problem.

Q8. What does a story need to contain before you are willing to start testing it, and what do you do when it arrives without those things?

Expected Answer

A strong candidate has a working definition of ready that goes beyond acceptance criteria. They typically want: clear acceptance criteria including negative and permission behaviour; the affected user roles; what happens to existing data when the change goes live; which environment and build contains the change; any configuration or feature flag needed to enable it; API contract changes if applicable; and dependencies on other teams or third parties.

The more revealing half is what they do when it is missing, because in practice it always is. Look for a graded response rather than a blanket refusal. They should describe reconstructing the missing context themselves where possible — reading the pull request or diff to see what actually changed, checking existing behaviour to infer intent, looking at how similar features already work, and asking the developer directly what they built and what they knowingly did not handle. Then they document their assumptions in the ticket so their interpretation is visible and correctable.

They should also be willing to push back proportionally: for a small change, proceed with assumptions noted; for a change touching money, permissions, or customer data, insist on clarification because the cost of guessing wrong is too high. That calibration is what distinguishes an experienced engineer from someone applying a rule.

Follow-up 1: The story says "validation should be added to the form." What do you ask?

Expected: Rapid, specific questioning. Which fields, and which are mandatory versus optional? What is valid for each — format, length limits, allowed character sets, numeric range? Is validation client-side, server-side, or both, and does the API enforce it independently, since a client-only rule is bypassable? What are the exact error messages and where do they appear? Is validation on blur, on submit, or as the user types? What happens to existing records that already violate the new rule? Does this apply to bulk import and API consumers as well as the form? A candidate who asks about existing invalid data and the API path is thinking about the system, not the screen.

Follow-up 2: How do you handle a story where the change is entirely backend with no visible UI?

Expected: They should be entirely comfortable here. Test at the API level against the contract; verify the database state directly; check logs and any emitted events or messages; confirm behaviour under retry and duplicate requests if it is an asynchronous or queue-driven change. For refactoring or performance work with no functional change intended, the test is that behaviour is provably identical — comparing responses before and after, running the existing regression suite, and checking response times. They should also ask what the change is meant to enable, because the real risk is usually in the consumers of that backend, not the backend itself.

Red Flags

  • Refuses to test anything without complete documentation, treating this as a principled stand.
  • Accepts every story as-is and discovers ambiguity only after finding a bug.
  • Has never read a pull request or looked at a code diff.
  • Cannot test anything that has no user interface.
  • Applies the same rigour to a label change as to a change in payment logic.

Strong Candidate Indicators

  • Asks about existing data migration and backward compatibility unprompted.
  • Distinguishes client-side from server-side validation and knows the client-side rule is not a control.
  • Reads the diff to scope the blast radius of a change rather than relying only on the ticket description.
  • Calibrates how hard to push based on business risk.

Q9. Your regression suite now takes longer than your sprint allows. Walk me through how you would fix that.

Expected Answer

A strong candidate starts by measuring rather than cutting. Which tests take the most time, which have never failed, which fail for non-product reasons, and which duplicate coverage that already exists at a lower level? Without that data, any reduction is guesswork.

Then they describe a layered strategy. Risk-tier the suite: a small critical set that runs on every build, a broader set nightly, and the full suite before release — so that speed and coverage are decoupled. Push tests down: a validation rule tested through fifteen UI cases can usually be tested once in the UI and fourteen times at API or unit level, which is both faster and more stable. Remove genuine duplication: multiple end-to-end tests that traverse login and navigation to assert one different thing each. Parallelise: more workers, sharded across CI containers — but they should note this requires test independence and unique test data, and does not fix a badly designed suite, it only makes it fail faster.

They should also address selection: running tests mapped to the changed area on pull requests, with the full suite reserved for release, and be honest that change-based selection is only as good as the mapping. Deleting tests should be on the table — a test that has never failed in eighteen months and duplicates lower-level coverage is a maintenance liability.

Follow-up 1: How would you decide which tests to delete, and how do you defend that decision?

Expected: Criteria, applied together rather than singly: has it ever failed for a real product reason; is the behaviour covered at a lower level; how business-critical is the path; how expensive is it to maintain and how often does it flake. Delete candidates are typically long UI tests over low-risk, rarely used screens whose logic is covered elsewhere. The defence is to write it down and share it before deleting — the coverage that is being removed, the layer where it still exists, and the risk accepted. A strong candidate mentions that flaky tests actively reduce suite value because a suite people ignore protects nothing.

Follow-up 2: You have parallelised and it is still too slow. What is left?

Expected: Move beyond the suite itself. Cut setup cost — authenticate once and reuse the stored session instead of logging in through the UI in every test; create preconditions by API instead of by clicking. Reduce environment latency and use a dedicated, appropriately sized test environment rather than a shared, contended one. Reconsider scope — whether the pre-release gate genuinely needs the full suite or whether a risk-based subset plus strong production monitoring and a fast rollback path is the better engineering trade-off. A candidate who reaches for "we accept less pre-release coverage because we can detect and revert in production within minutes" is thinking at the right altitude.

Red Flags

  • Only answer is "run it in parallel" with no attention to test design or independence.
  • Believes every test ever written must be preserved forever.
  • Wants to automate everything at the UI level and sees no role for API or unit coverage.
  • Has no idea which of their tests are slowest or which fail most often.
  • Tolerates a suite with known flaky failures and a manual "just re-run it" habit.

Strong Candidate Indicators

  • Measures before optimising, and can quote real numbers from their own suite.
  • Actively moves coverage down the stack rather than adding at the top.
  • Has deleted tests deliberately and can explain the reasoning and how they socialised it.
  • Understands that suite trustworthiness matters more than suite size.

Q10. How do you decide the severity and priority of a bug? Give me a case where the two did not match.

Expected Answer

A strong candidate separates the two cleanly: severity is the technical impact on the system when the bug occurs; priority is how soon the business needs it fixed. They should then produce a real mismatch in each direction, which is where genuine experience shows.

High severity, low priority: a crash in an administrative bulk-export screen used by two internal users once a quarter, with a straightforward manual workaround. The failure is total when it happens, but the business impact of deferring it is small. Low severity, high priority: a spelling mistake in the company name on the customer-facing invoice, or a wrong currency symbol on the pricing page — cosmetically trivial, functionally harmless, but it damages credibility and is visible to every customer, so it goes out in the next hotfix.

They should explain the inputs they actually use: number and type of users affected, frequency, whether data or money is corrupted, whether a workaround exists and how painful it is, regulatory or contractual exposure, and reputational visibility. A mature answer acknowledges that QA proposes severity from evidence and the product owner owns priority, and that the QA engineer's job is to supply the impact data that makes the priority call correct rather than to argue for their own ranking.

Follow-up 1: You find a bug that corrupts data silently but affects only a small number of records. How do you argue its priority?

Expected: They should escalate hard and explain why: silent corruption compounds. Every hour it runs adds records requiring cleanup, wrong data flows into reports and downstream systems, and the eventual remediation cost is far higher than the fix. Strong candidates raise the questions that matter to the decision: how many records so far, is it still occurring, can we identify and correct the affected records, and has bad data already left the system into a warehouse, a partner feed, or a filed report. They may recommend disabling the affected path immediately even before a fix exists — stopping the bleeding first.

Follow-up 2: How do you handle disagreement when the product owner sets a priority you think is wrong?

Expected: Make the case once, with evidence, in writing — affected user count, frequency, worst-case consequence, remediation cost if deferred — then accept the decision and ensure it is recorded. If the risk is regulatory, security-related, or involves data integrity, a strong candidate says they would escalate beyond the product owner, because those categories are not purely a product prioritisation call. Look for someone who understands the difference between advocating and obstructing, and who does not keep relitigating a settled decision.

Red Flags

  • Uses severity and priority interchangeably, or defines them with textbook wording but cannot produce an example.
  • Every bug they find is critical.
  • Cannot give a real mismatch case from their own work.
  • Ignores workaround availability entirely when assessing impact.
  • Treats priority as something QA decides.

Strong Candidate Indicators

  • Quantifies impact — affected users, frequency, records — instead of describing it qualitatively.
  • Recognises silent data corruption as a category deserving different treatment.
  • Knows which categories justify escalation past normal prioritisation.
  • Provides the data that makes someone else's decision better rather than defending their own label.

Q11. You are asked how much testing a feature will take, and you have never tested anything like it. How do you estimate?

Expected Answer

Look for structured decomposition and honesty about uncertainty. A strong candidate breaks the work into parts they can reason about: how many distinct user roles, how many states or workflow stages, how many integration points, how much new test data must be created, whether automation is expected within the same sprint, and how much environment or setup work is required before testing can even begin.

They should identify unknowns explicitly and propose reducing them before committing: a short spike to explore the feature or the third-party sandbox, then estimating with real information. Giving a range with stated assumptions is stronger than a single confident number — "three to five days, assuming the third-party sandbox is available and I do not need to build new test data for the settlement flow; if either of those is false, add two days."

They should include the parts inexperienced estimators forget: environment setup, test data creation, retesting after fixes, and regression impact on adjacent areas. Retest time in particular is routinely omitted and is frequently the largest hidden cost. A mature candidate also mentions calibrating against past work — comparing to a similar feature and how long that actually took, not how long it was estimated to take.

Follow-up 1: Your estimate was half of what it actually took. What do you do during the sprint, and what do you do afterwards?

Expected: Raise it as soon as the trend is visible, not at the deadline — "I am two days in on a three-day estimate and about forty percent through; the settlement flow has more states than I expected." Then present options rather than only a problem: reduce scope of coverage, get help, extend, or defer part to the next sprint with stated risk. Afterwards, examine the reason: was the feature genuinely larger, was the environment unreliable, was there more rework than expected, or was the estimate simply optimistic? Feeding that back into the next estimate is the whole point.

Follow-up 2: How much of your estimate is retesting bugs, and how do you account for something you cannot predict?

Expected: An experienced engineer has a rough empirical figure — often a quarter to a third of the testing effort on a substantial new feature goes into retest and verification cycles — and derives it from observation rather than a formula. They should explain that retest cost is driven by defect density and by how fixes arrive: several small fixes trickling in each requiring partial regression is far more expensive than one batched fix. They may mention that a feature with unstable early builds should be flagged early, since high initial defect density reliably predicts an overrun.

Red Flags

  • Gives a number instantly with no decomposition or stated assumptions.
  • Never accounts for retesting, environment setup, or test data creation.
  • Has never had an estimate be wrong.
  • Reports overrun only at the deadline.
  • Treats estimation as a number handed to them by a lead rather than something they reason about.

Strong Candidate Indicators

  • Estimates in ranges with explicit assumptions, and proposes a spike to reduce uncertainty.
  • Includes retest, data setup, and regression impact as named line items.
  • Calibrates against actuals from previous work, not against previous estimates.
  • Escalates variance early with options attached.

Q12. Your team wants to release every week instead of every three weeks. What has to change on the QA side for that to be safe?

Expected Answer

A strong candidate recognises that the constraint is not testing speed but feedback speed, and that the answer is structural rather than a matter of working faster.

Expect several of these: automated regression that runs unattended on every build and is trusted, because a weekly manual regression cycle is arithmetically impossible; a fast, reliable pipeline with results in tens of minutes, since a suite taking four hours cannot gate a weekly release; feature flags so incomplete work can merge and ship dark, decoupling deployment from release — and the corresponding testing implication that flag combinations become part of the test matrix; smaller, independently shippable changes, which reduce both the risk and the testing surface per release; production observability — error rate dashboards, alerting on key business metrics, log aggregation — so problems are detected in minutes rather than by customer complaint; and a genuinely fast rollback or flag-off path, tested rather than assumed.

The strongest candidates make the philosophical shift explicit: at weekly cadence you deliberately move some risk detection from pre-release to post-release, and that is only responsible if detection and recovery are fast. They should also note what constrains this in enterprise contexts — database migrations, mobile app store review cycles, partner integrations, and change-approval or compliance processes that may not permit weekly deployment regardless of technical readiness.

Follow-up 1: What would you refuse to move out of the pre-release gate, no matter how good your monitoring is?

Expected: Anything where post-release detection is too late because the damage is irreversible or unacceptable. Typically: authentication and authorisation, because a permission leak cannot be undone once data has been exposed; payment and financial calculation, because incorrect money movement requires reconciliation and possibly regulatory reporting; anything that writes or migrates customer data destructively; and regulatory or audit-relevant behaviour. A strong candidate articulates the underlying principle — monitoring can substitute for testing only where the failure is detectable, reversible, and bounded.

Follow-up 2: Six months in, the team is releasing weekly but the automation suite is flaky and half-trusted. What do you do?

Expected: They should recognise this as the actual failure mode of most acceleration efforts, and treat it as the top priority rather than something to work around. Concretely: quarantine flaky tests so they stop polluting the signal, but with an explicit owner and deadline rather than indefinite exile; measure per-test failure rates to find the worst offenders; fix root causes — usually waits, shared test data, or test interdependence — rather than adding retries; and be blunt with the team that a suite people routinely re-run until green provides no gate at all. A candidate who says "the suite must be trusted or the cadence is unsafe" and means it is showing real engineering judgement.

Red Flags

  • Answers only "we need more automation" with no view on pipeline speed, reliability, or observability.
  • No concept of feature flags or of separating deployment from release.
  • Believes full manual regression can be repeated weekly.
  • Ignores rollback and monitoring entirely.
  • Sees no category of risk that must stay in the pre-release gate.

Strong Candidate Indicators

  • Frames the problem as feedback and recovery time, not testing throughput.
  • Explicitly reasons about which risks can move to post-release and which cannot, with a stated principle.
  • Raises the real-world constraints — migrations, mobile release cycles, compliance approvals.
  • Treats suite trustworthiness as a precondition rather than an aspiration.
